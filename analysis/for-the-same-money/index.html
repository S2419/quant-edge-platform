Quant Lab · 13 October 2026

For the same money, would we have bought better?

We rebuilt 120 clubs' summer 2024 windows with their own budgets. Our picks did better. The clubs' own signings were hard to tell apart from a random pick of players they could afford.

  • Our picks out-produced 75% of the players in their position in their division, on average. The clubs' real signings: 60%. A random pick of players they could afford: 57%.
  • Cheaper too: about 60% of what the clubs spent on the same slots, roughly €2.0bn against €3.3bn. That wasn't a goal. The rule only had to stay inside the budget.
  • We expected a wash. We wrote that down before running it. It wasn't one.

The question

Every club makes the same bet each summer: with this money, for these positions, who do we sign? You can't rerun a transfer window. You can rebuild one on paper, using a model that learned from the previous summer only, and see how the alternative would have done.

We wrote the rules down and committed them to git on 6 October, before building a single alternative squad. The rules were not changed after the results came in.

The rules

  • Our signing model learned from summer 2023 alone. No 2024 result touched it.
  • Every club that paid a reported fee in summer 2024 and had a workable alternative (120 club-windows) gets the budget it actually spent on its outfield signings, and must sign the same number of defenders, midfielders and attackers it did.
  • Who it could have bought: every other summer 2024 signing with a reported fee who joined a club of similar strength to the buyer, on our rating as it stood on 1 June 2024. A player who joined a far stronger club was not realistically on a smaller club's list, and the reverse. The typical club had 138 to choose from.
  • Our picks: the players we rated highest that fit the budget and the positions.
  • The random pick: 200 random squads per club from the same affordable list, same positions, same budget.

How we scored it

Each signing gets one number: the share of players in his position, in his division, that season, that he out-produced. Players level with him count as half. 50% is the middle. 75% means he did more than about three in four of them.

An example. A Championship attacker on 75% had more goals plus assists that season than 75% of the Championship attackers who played.

  • Attackers: goals plus assists.
  • Midfielders: goals, assists and key passes.
  • Defenders: tackles, interceptions and duels won.

It counts season totals at the club he joined, not rates per 90. A signing who never played lands near the bottom. Flops stay in.

We average that number over each club's signings, then over the 120 clubs. A gap of 15 points means our picks out-produced 15 more players in every 100 in their position. On a list of 100, that is about 15 places higher, on average.

  • Ours against the clubs: 15 points better, with a 95% range of 10 to 20 points. Our picks beat the club's own in 89 of 120 windows.
  • Ours against random: 18 points better, range 16 to 21. This is the comparison that matters. If a random squad had beaten the clubs by as much, the gain would have come from the rules, not from our ratings.
  • The clubs against random: 4 points better, range from just below zero to 8. Clubs beat the random pick in 70 of 120 windows.
  • Weighted by money spent: 77% ours, 60% clubs, 58% random. Same picture. This was in the plan, and we computed it afterwards from the registered per-club results.

What the clubs' number means, and what it doesn't

On this test, in this window, we can't tell the clubs' choices apart from a random pick of players they could afford.

That is not the same as saying recruitment is random. Clubs pay wages we can't see, sign for reasons a season total doesn't capture, and know who would actually come. One window is not much. We say "can't tell apart", not "no better".

It didn't improve with more signings. For the 44 clubs that made three or more paid signings, the real intake scored 56% against 57% for random. We decided before the test that this split would decide nothing, and it doesn't.

Why a weak model wins this

Our ratings aren't sharp. Across these signings, what we predicted and what happened line up only loosely: a correlation of about 0.27.

That turns out to be enough. Pick the best one or two in each position out of dozens on a rough guide and you still land well above the average of the list. The gain comes from a big choice and a consistent rule, not a brilliant forecast.

The catch we looked for

We expected a wash, so we went looking for a flaw. The obvious one: a player's number was earned at the club he actually joined. If our picks had all joined stronger clubs, they'd be borrowing their teammates' quality.

It runs the other way. Our picks joined clubs about 30 rating points weaker than the buyer on our club-strength rating, against about 13 weaker for the random picks. So they weren't borrowing stronger teammates. Across all these signings, the strength of the club a player joined and his number are barely related (-0.01), because the number is measured inside his own division.

That rules out one catch and points at another. A player at a weaker club is more likely to start, and season totals reward minutes. Whether our picks would have played as much at the buyer is the assumption this test can't check.

What this test can't tell you

  • We rated each player where he actually went. Our rating used the club he joined: its strength, the minutes open there, who else it signed that summer. It did not rate him for the buyer's squad. Some of that was only known once the window shut.
  • No wages. A club's real limit is fees plus wages, and we have no wage data.
  • Fees for a small slice. Reported fees cover about 9% of moves, mostly the bigger deals. This tests the visible end of the market.
  • Who could sign whom is a guess. We only know a player was for sale, and at what price, because somebody bought him. Selling clubs pick buyers, players pick clubs, and agents get involved.
  • It assumes the player would have done as well at the buyer. A player who starts every week at a weaker club might not at a stronger one, and totals reward minutes.
  • One window. Our feed stopped reporting fees after summer 2024, so no later window can be tested with a budget.
  • Defending is counted, not judged. Tackles and interceptions reward ball-winners over positionally good defenders.
  • No goalkeepers. Our data has no saves or clean sheets.

What it's for

Not a shopping list. A club's own record. Run on one club's recent windows, using that club's own fee data, the same test asks whether its recruitment added anything over the players it could have afforded. With five to ten signings a window, most clubs will land in "can't tell", and we'll say so. What it finds is the clear outliers, either way.

Data sources

  • API-Football: transfers and reported fees, player season statistics, league fixtures and results.
  • Club strength: our own rating, built from those results.
  • The rules were committed to git on 6 October 2026, before any result was seen.

Re-run on 13 October 2026 on current data: our picks changed for 8 of the 120 clubs, and the gap over the clubs came out at 15.1 points instead of 14.9. The figures above are from the original, registered run.
