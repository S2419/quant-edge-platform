Which football stats actually mean anything

Quant Edge Syndicate · 17 September 2026

Football analysis runs on per-season numbers. Conversion rate, shot accuracy, dribble success, duels won. They get quoted as though they describe a player.

Some of them do. Most of them describe a season.

So we tested them, one at a time. The method is simple enough to describe in four steps.

  1. Rank every Premier League player on a stat. Passes per game, say.
  2. Cut the list in half. Top half, bottom half.
  3. Measure how far apart those two groups are.
  4. Wait a year, find the same players, and measure the gap between the same two groups again.

If the gap is still there, the stat was telling you about the players. If the gap has closed, it was telling you about the season they happened to have.

We call what is left the retention. A stat that keeps 83% of its gap is describing something durable. One that keeps 11% has watched its two groups collapse into each other.

The one correction that changes everything

Compare a striker with a centre-back and of course the striker takes more shots. He will next year too. That is not a stat being reliable, it is just a centre-back still being a centre-back.

So every comparison here happens inside a position group: forwards against forwards, defenders against defenders. It matters more than you would guess. Lumped together, shots per 90 looks 89% reliable. Done properly, 64%. Most of the difference was positions, not persistence.

The specification. Four completed Premier League seasons, 2022/23 to 2025/26, giving three consecutive-season comparisons. Every outfielder with 600 or more minutes in both halves of a comparison: 663 players, 652 season-pairs. Split at the median, measured within position and weighted back together.

What a player does persists. How well he does it barely does.

What these numbers are not. Retention is one specific thing: the gap between two groups this year, divided by the gap between the same two groups last year.

It is not a correlation. It is not the share of variance explained. And it is not an estimate of how much of a stat is skill — “only 11% of finishing persists” sounds like a measurement of skill and is not one.

We report correlation separately, in its own column, because it answers a different question: not “is the gap still there” but “are the same players still ahead”. On conversion the two land at 11% and 0.10, which is close enough to be confusing and is pure coincidence.

The top of the table: what a player does

Everything near the top is a count of how often something happens. A player who tried a lot of dribbles this season tries a lot next season, at a correlation of 0.90, the highest figure on the board. Passing volume, duels, key passes and tackles all hold 70% of their gap or better.

These are habits. They describe how someone plays and what his manager asks of him, and neither changes much in twelve months.

The bottom of the table: how well he does it

Everything near the bottom is a success rate, and success rates fall apart. Shot accuracy keeps 13% of its gap. Dribble success keeps 31%. Conversion — goals per shot on target — keeps 11%, with a year-on-year correlation of 0.10.

One caveat that matters, and it cuts against us. That 11% covers every outfielder, centre-backs included, and a finishing rate means very little for a centre-back. Among attackers alone it rises to 23%, on 130 season-pairs. Still far below every volume stat on the board. But if the question is about forwards, 23% is the honest number to quote, not 11%.

What that looks like with real players

Take Premier League attackers and split them on finishing alone: the good half and the poor half, 92 consecutive season-pairs.

In year one the good half converted 46.6% of their shots on target. The poor half converted 27.7%. A year later the good half had fallen to 37.1% and the poor half had climbed to 33.1%. Both ended up either side of the league median of 34.8%, four percentage points apart.

Nobody moved clubs to cause that. Nobody changed as a player. Both groups simply drifted back towards the middle, which is what happens to numbers that were partly luck the first time.

A 19.0-point gap became a 4.0-point gap. Roughly four fifths of it was gone inside a year. The 95% interval on that change runs from −20.0 to −9.8 points, which is nowhere near zero, so this is not something we found by staring at noise.

One player, both halves

Erling Haaland shows the split better than any chart, because the two halves of his game behave completely differently.

How often he shoots barely moves. Across four seasons: 3.47, 3.45, 3.02, 3.10 shots per 90. That is roughly double a typical Premier League attacker, and even his quietest year sits more than 80% above that benchmark.

How often those shots go in swings wildly. 60%, then 46%, then 37%, then 46% of his shots on target converted.

His goal rate fell by nearly 40% between 2022/23 and 2024/25 — 1.17 per 90 down to 0.72 — while his shooting hardly changed. Nothing about how he plays broke. The part that moved was the part that was never steady in the first place.

What we are not claiming

This is not a finding that finishing is a myth. Over a career, some players are plainly better at it. The claim is narrower: a single season of conversion rate carries very little information about the next one.

And some of this collapse is guaranteed by arithmetic, not by football. Every measurement carries noise. Whenever you take the top half of a noisy measurement, you have partly selected the players who got lucky — and luck does not repeat. So the top half always falls back a bit, for every stat, even a perfectly good one.

That is exactly why the ranking is what carries meaning here, not the individual percentages. Every stat in the table faces the same headwind. Passing volume keeps 83% of its gap anyway. Conversion keeps 11%. The distance between those two is real, even if both numbers are flattered by the same arithmetic.

And this is one league, four seasons, public per-season aggregates. No event data, no tracking. A centre-back and a full-back are both filed as "Defender" in this feed, which limits any position-specific reading.

What this might mean for recruitment

We are not claiming this improves anyone's recruitment decisions. We have not tested it against signings and outcomes, and until someone does, any claim in that direction is unearned.

What we can say is narrower. If a screen ranks forwards on conversion rate, most of what separates the top of that list from the bottom will not be there next season. The same goes for shot accuracy at 13%. The metrics that survive are the boring ones: how often he shoots, not how often he scores; how often he takes a man on, not how often it comes off.

Whether that matters depends on how much weight those metrics carry in your own process, which we cannot see from here. If you rank players for a living, we would genuinely like to know whether this is relevant to how you work, and whether the method holds up against data better than ours.

Reproduction

Every number here comes from our own code against a per-season player panel collected from a public API. Method, filters and code are available on request, including the parts that did not work.

We publish negative results as a matter of course. It is the only reason to believe the positive ones.
