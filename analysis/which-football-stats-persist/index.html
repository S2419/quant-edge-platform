Which football stats actually mean anything

Quant Edge Syndicate · 17 September 2026

Football analysis runs on per-season numbers. Conversion rate, shot accuracy, dribble success, duels won. They get quoted as though they describe a player.

Some of them do. Most of them describe a season.

We tested which is which. Five Premier League seasons, every outfielder with 600 or more minutes, 663 consecutive season-pairs. For each metric we split the players at the median and measured how much of the gap between the two halves was still there a year later, on the same players.

Crucially, that comparison is made within position. Pooled across everyone, a large part of the gap on something like shot volume is simply forwards being forwards, and a centre-back is still a centre-back next season. Measured pooled, shots per 90 looks 86% stable. Measured properly, it is 63%.

What a player does persists. How well he does it barely does.

Read the top

Volume and behaviour hold up. A player who attempts a lot of dribbles this season attempts a lot next season, at a correlation of 0.88, the highest on the board. Passing volume, duels, key passes and tackles all sit at 69% or better.

These are traits. They describe how a player plays and what he is asked to do, and that is stable.

Read the bottom

Efficiency collapses. Shot accuracy retains 13%. Dribble success 32%. Conversion, meaning goals per shot on target, retains 8% with a year-on-year correlation of 0.08.

One caveat that matters. That 8% is measured across all outfielders, including centre-backs for whom a finishing rate is close to meaningless. Restricted to attackers alone, with a 20-shot filter, it rises to roughly 19%. Still far below every volume metric, but the honest number for a question about forwards is the higher one.

The clearest case

The same test on Premier League attackers, split on conversion, 92 consecutive season-pairs. The good finishers fell from 0.466 to 0.371. The poor finishers rose from 0.277 to 0.331. Both landed either side of the league median of 0.350, within 0.04 of each other.

A separation of 0.189 becomes 0.040, so roughly four fifths of it is gone in a season. The 95% interval on that change is −0.201 to −0.096, which does not come near zero.

Haaland

Erling Haaland is the clearest single illustration, because the two halves of him behave completely differently.

His shooting barely moves: 3.47, 3.45, 3.02 and 3.10 shots per 90 across four seasons, roughly double the league median, and even his lowest year is 65% above average. His finishing swings by sixty per cent: 60%, then 46%, then 37%, then 46% of shots on target converted.

His goal rate fell by a third between 2022/23 and 2024/25 while his shot volume barely changed. Nothing about how he plays broke. The part that moved was the part that was never stable to begin with.

What we are not claiming

This is not a finding that finishing is a myth. Over a career, some players are plainly better at it. The claim is narrower: a single season of conversion rate carries very little information about the next one.

Part of the regression is also mechanical. Split any measure containing error and the top half falls back, which is arithmetic rather than football. That is precisely why the comparison between metrics carries the meaning here. Every metric in the table is measured with error, and passing volume retains 82% anyway while conversion retains 8%.

And this is one league, five seasons, public per-season aggregates. No event data, no tracking. A centre-back and a full-back are both filed as "Defender" in this feed, which limits any position-specific reading.

What it implies for recruitment

A screen that ranks forwards on conversion rate is ranking them largely on last season's variance. The same goes for shot accuracy at 13%.

The metrics that survive are the boring ones. How often he shoots, not how often he scores. How often he takes a man on, not how often it comes off.
