What a season of 23 assists is actually worth

Last season Christos Tzolis produced 23 league assists for Club Brugge. He is now at Arsenal. How much of that number travels with him?

This piece is the method from our earlier work pointed at one player, in public, while the answer is still unknown. We would rather be checkable than right.

The split

Our persistence research found a clean divide across 652 Premier League season-pairs. Counts of how often a player does something hold 64–84% of their year-one gap twelve months later. Success rates hold 11–31%.

Every attacking season splits along that line.

  1. How many chances he created. That is a habit. It describes his role and how he plays.
  2. How many of those chances got finished. That depends on the player at the other end of the pass.

An assist needs both. Which is why an assist total is a strange thing to buy a player on: half of it is a report on his teammates.

The half that looks real

His chance creation has risen every single season: 1.74, then 2.45, then 3.92 key passes per 90. That is the most persistent kind of stat we measured — key passes hold 78% of their gap year to year — and it is still climbing.

Worth being careful here, in the opposite direction to the rest of this piece. A rising line is not the same thing as a stable one. Our retention figures describe players whose level holds steady, not players still developing. For someone improving this fast, the average of his past seasons understates him, and nobody knows yet where the line flattens out.

The half that leans on other people

Now the success rate. Of the chances Tzolis created, what share became goals?

His teammates converted his chances at 1.41 times the rate they had managed across his previous two seasons.

Run last season again at his own earlier rate and those 134 key passes produce roughly 16 assists, not 23. On that arithmetic, about seven of his assists came from the finishing at the other end rather than from him.

The creation is his. A good part of the assist total belongs to whoever was on the end of it.

Why we cannot prove this for him

This is the honest limit, and it is a real one.

One player's season is around 130 chances. That is a small sample, and small samples produce wide margins of error. We cannot show that Tzolis individually was lucky, and anyone claiming otherwise from one season of data is overreaching.

What we have is the population. Across 652 season-pairs, success rates behave like this as a rule. So the claim is actuarial, not predictive:

Players whose success rate jumps this far above their own established level usually give most of it back. By that standard, this much of the 23 looks like the spike rather than the player. That is a statement about the population, not a forecast for one man.

An insurer prices a house without predicting which one burns down. Same logic, same limits.

A closed case, to check the method

Tzolis is unfinished. Here is one that is not.

In 2023/24 Dominic Solanke scored 19 Premier League goals for Bournemouth. Tottenham paid a reported £65m for him that August, Bournemouth's record sale. Using only what was visible beforehand:

The habit held almost exactly: 1.03 shots on target per 90 in the season that sold him, 1.06 in his first season afterwards. He kept getting into the same positions and getting the shot away just as often.

The success rate did not. 50% in the selling season, 35% after the move, against the 21% he had shown the year before.

And here is the part we will not skip. That 35% comes off 26 shots on target, which carries a 95% interval from 19% to 54%. The selling season's 50% sits inside that range. So even in a case that looks this clean, the drop is not established for Solanke as an individual. It is consistent with the population finding. It does not prove it on its own.

His 2025/26 is excluded entirely: an ankle injury in pre-season, surgery, fifteen appearances. Injury cuts the sample rather than the rate, and three goals from eleven shots on target measures nothing at all.

What this is not. Not a verdict on a transfer. Fee, fit, role, minutes, injuries and the rest sit outside this and all of them matter more than anything here. The only claim is that the number the market reacted to was part habit and part spike, and that the split was visible in advance.

What we are betting on, in public

Tzolis has played 211 Premier League minutes across three appearances. He is creating 3.41 chances per 90, against 3.92 last season. His teammates have converted 12.5% of them, against 17.2% last season and 12.2% before that.

Three substitute appearances prove nothing whatsoever. But note the difference from Solanke's excluded season: those minutes are small because it is September, not because anyone is unavailable. This season accumulates. By May it is a real test.

Our position, written down now rather than after the fact: the chance creation should largely survive the step up, and the assist rate per chance should settle closer to 12% than 17%. If both hold, the method worked on a case we called in advance. If the creation collapses, we were wrong about the more important half.

Method

League football only. Retention figures from 652 Premier League season-pairs across 2022/23 to 2025/26, measured within position. Player seasons from a public per-season API, no event or tracking data. Intervals are Wilson intervals on the underlying counts.

We publish the cases that go against us too. It is the only reason to believe the ones that do not.
