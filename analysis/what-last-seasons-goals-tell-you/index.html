The half of a striker's season worth paying for

A striker scored fifteen last season. How much should that change what you think of him?

We expected this piece to say “less than you think”. It does not, and we were wrong in a way worth writing down.

The trap we walked into

Our first piece found that finishing barely lasts. Split players into good and poor finishers, come back a year later, and almost all of the difference between them has evaporated.

From which there is an obvious next step, and we took it. If goals are part shooting and part finishing, and finishing is mostly luck, then stop counting goals. Count shots instead. Shots are the clean signal underneath.

It sounds right. We tested it anyway. Among Premier League attackers, last season's goals predict next season's goals slightly better than shot volume does.

Goals score 0.53, goals and assists 0.49, shots 0.47. But look at the whiskers: those three overlap almost completely. The honest reading is not that goals win. It is that all three tell you about the same amount, and none of them beats the others by enough to bother swapping.

Swapping goals for shots does not buy you anything. The mistake is not which number you use. It is taking that number apart and paying extra for the wrong half.

Two questions that sound like one

This is the bit that took us longest to see, so here it is slowly.

A class sits an exam. The top half average 80%, the bottom half 50%. A year later the same class sits a much harder paper and everyone's marks fall. Now the top half average 60% and the bottom half still average 50%.

The gap between the two groups has collapsed, from thirty points to ten. And yet the same pupils are still the ones at the top.

“Is the gap still there?” and “are the same people still ahead?” are different questions. A stat can fail the first and pass the second.

Our first piece measured the gap. This one measures the running order. A striker's goal rate does fall back hard towards the pack — that is the first piece, and it is true — and he is still roughly where he was in the queue.

So what should you throw away?

Not the goals. The success rates hiding inside them.

Conversion — the share of his shots on target that went in — scores 0.23 against next season's goals. Shot accuracy scores 0.08, and its interval crosses zero, which is the statistical way of saying we cannot rule out that it tells you nothing whatsoever.

Those two are exactly what a scouting report reaches for when it wants to justify a signing the plain goal count does not support. He is a natural finisher. His shot accuracy is elite. Neither claim survives the following season.

What it means for a shortlist

Split a striker's season in two.

How often he got a shot away is a habit. It describes how he plays and what his team asks of him, and it shows up again next year.

How often those shots went in is mostly the season he happened to have.

Here is the practical version. Keep the goal count. It quietly contains both halves, and it does the job as well as anything else we tested. What you should not do is prise it apart, find the finishing half, and pay a premium for that specifically.

One thing we did not expect

Among attackers, key passes per 90 scores minus 0.15 against next season's goals. Slightly negative. The players creating the chances are not the players finishing them.

The interval crosses zero, so we are not calling it a finding. But it does undercut any product that rolls a forward's whole game into a single rating, because at least two of the ingredients point in opposite directions.

Limits

130 attacker season-pairs is not many. The three leading stats sit inside each other's intervals, and we have said so rather than ranking them as though the order meant something.

This is one league, four completed seasons, and public season totals. No event data, no tracking. And correlation here is about who ranks where, not about how many goals any particular player will score.

Most of all: 0.53 still leaves the large majority of next season unexplained. The claim is that goals are the best of a weak field. Not that they are good.
