The half of a striker's season worth paying for

A striker scored fifteen last season. How much should that change what you think of him?

More than we expected. We ran this test assuming our own earlier research pointed the other way, and it did not.

The obvious conclusion, and why it is wrong

Our first piece found that finishing barely persists. Split players into good and bad finishers, come back a year later, and almost all of the gap between them has gone.

So the obvious move is to stop looking at goals and start looking at shots. Goals are contaminated by finishing, finishing is mostly luck, and shot volume is the clean trait underneath.

We tested it. Among Premier League attackers, last season's goal rate predicts next season's goal rate better than shot volume does.

Goals at 0.53, goals and assists at 0.49, shots at 0.47. Those three intervals sit on top of each other, so the honest reading is that all three tell you roughly the same thing. What they do not do is beat each other.

Swapping goals for shots does not buy you anything. The mistake is not which number you use. It is taking that number apart and paying extra for the wrong half.

Why persistence did not mean prediction

This is the part that took us a while, so it is worth going slowly.

Imagine a class sits an exam. The top half average 80%, the bottom half average 50%. A year later the same class sits a harder paper. Everyone's marks fall. Now the top half average 60% and the bottom half still average 50%.

The gap between the groups has collapsed from thirty points to ten. But the same pupils are still the ones at the top.

Those are two different questions with two different answers. "Is the gap still there?" is not the same question as "are the same people still ahead?"

Our first piece measured the gap. This one measures the order. A striker's goal rate falls back hard towards the pack, which is exactly what the first chart shows, and he is still mostly in the same place in the queue.

So what should you throw away?

Not the goals. The efficiency inside them.

Conversion, meaning goals per shot on target, correlates 0.23 with next season's goals. Shot accuracy correlates 0.08, and its interval crosses zero, which means we cannot rule out that it tells you nothing at all.

Those are the two numbers a scouting report reaches for when it wants to justify a signing the raw goal count does not support. He is a natural finisher. His shot accuracy is elite. Neither survives contact with the following season.

What it means for a shortlist

Take a striker's season and break it in two.

How often he got a shot away is a trait. It describes how he plays and what his team asks of him, and it turns up again next year.

How often those shots went in is mostly the season he had.

Keep the goal count. It quietly contains both halves and it does the job as well as anything else we tested. What you should not do is take it apart, find the finishing half, and pay a premium for that.

One thing we did not expect

Among attackers, key passes per 90 correlates minus 0.15 with next season's goals. The players creating chances are not the players finishing them.

The interval crosses zero so we are not claiming it as a finding. But it does undercut any pitch that rolls a forward's whole game into one number, because two of the components point in opposite directions.

Limits

130 attacker season-pairs is not many. The three leading metrics are inside each other's intervals and we have said so rather than ranking them.

This is one league, four completed seasons, and public per-season aggregates. No event data and no tracking. Correlation here is about who ranks where, not about how many goals a given player will score.

And a correlation of 0.53 still leaves most of next season unexplained. The claim is that goals are the best of a weak field, not that they are good.
